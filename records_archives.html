Start Here: Where Did the Event or Record Happen?

Most records are held by the place where the event happened, the court that handled the matter, the office that recorded the property, the military/archive agency that kept the file, or the country/territory that created the document.

Best first question: What type of record do I need, and where did it happen?

What to Gather Before You Contact an Office

Basic details

  • Full name and any maiden, married, previous, or alternate names
  • Date or approximate year of the event
  • City, county, state, territory, or country where it happened
  • Parents, spouse, child, or next-of-kin names if known
  • Reason you need the record and whether you need a certified copy

Proof and permission

  • Your photo ID or official account login when required
  • Relationship to the person named in the record
  • Court order, authorization, or proof of legal authority if needed
  • Payment method for official fees
  • Mailing address and contact information

For older or family-history records

  • Approximate dates instead of exact dates
  • Religion/church, cemetery, military branch, immigration port, or property location
  • Old maps, family Bible pages, letters, photos, obituaries, or family notes
  • County changes, border changes, name changes, and language spelling variations

Record Types: Where to Begin

Birth, death, marriage & divorce

Start with the vital records office, civil registry, health department, courthouse, county clerk, town hall, state/territory office, or country office where the event occurred. For certified copies, ask what ID, relationship proof, fee, and form are required.

Adoption records

Adoption records are often restricted and rules vary by state, territory, country, court, and age of the person requesting. Start with the court, state adoption agency, child welfare agency, vital records office, or legal aid.

Cemetery, burial & obituary records

Start with the cemetery office, funeral home, local library, newspaper archive, church/faith community, county death index, historical society, or local genealogy society.

Court, probate & estate records

Start with the local courthouse, probate court, circuit/superior court, county clerk, register of wills, or court records office where the case or estate was handled.

Military records

For U.S. service records, start with the National Archives / National Personnel Records Center. For other countries, start with that country’s defense ministry, military archives, national archives, veterans agency, or embassy/consulate.

Immigration & naturalization

For U.S. historical records, start with National Archives and USCIS Genealogy. For other countries, check immigration, citizenship, civil registry, national archives, embassy, or consulate pathways.

Property, land & deed records

Start with the county recorder, register of deeds, land registry, tax assessor, survey office, courthouse, state archives, or national land records office. For U.S. federal land patents, check BLM General Land Office records.

Genealogy, archives & historical records

Start with national archives, state archives, territory archives, libraries, historical societies, genealogy centers, church/parish registers, cemetery records, census records, newspapers, maps, and family papers.

Tribal / Indigenous records

Start respectfully with the tribe/nation’s own enrollment or records office when applicable. Also check national archives, local history guides, BIA-related pathways, libraries, and court/land records. Do not assume DNA testing alone proves tribal enrollment or legal status.

Family History, African American Genealogy & Native American Records

African American Genealogy

Start with official and historical collections such as the National Archives, Library of Congress, Smithsonian National Museum of African American History and Culture, Freedmen’s Bureau records, census records, military records, church records, newspapers, cemeteries, state archives, local libraries, and historical societies. Record names and access may vary by place and time period.

Native American Records

Start respectfully with the tribe or nation’s enrollment, history, or records office when appropriate. Additional starting points may include the National Archives, Library of Congress, Bureau of Indian Affairs pathways, state archives, local libraries, court records, land records, and official tribal resources. DNA results alone do not establish tribal citizenship or enrollment.

Government Archives

For older government records, start with national, federal, state, provincial, territory, county, municipal, or local archives and libraries. Ask which office holds the record type and date range you need.

International Archives

For records outside the United States, start with the country’s national archives, civil registry, ministry, provincial or regional archive, municipal office, court, land registry, library, embassy, or consulate. Use official country sources first and ask about language, access, certification, translation, and authentication requirements.

Medical, Hospital, Immunization, Education & Employment Records

Medical, hospital & immunization records

Start with the provider, hospital, clinic, health department, patient portal, medical records or Health Information Management office, immunization registry, Ministry of Health, or national health system that created or stores the record. Ask what identification, authorization, fee, and release form are required.

School, college & transcript records

Start with the school, district, registrar, records office, closed-school custodian, state or territory education agency, ministry of education, or official qualifications authority. Use the Education Records page for detailed pathways.

Employment & training records

Start with the employer, Human Resources office, payroll office, training provider, apprenticeship sponsor, licensing board, school or program, placement office, or government employment agency that created or regulates the record.

DNA Resources — Commercial Services

Commercial notice: Consumer DNA testing and private genealogy platforms are commercial services, not government record offices. Fees, privacy practices, databases, matching tools, terms, and availability vary.

Before using a commercial DNA service, review its privacy policy, data-sharing choices, account-deletion process, law-enforcement policy, family-impact considerations, and whether results are accepted for the purpose you need. DNA testing does not replace certified vital records, court orders, legal proof, immigration documentation, or tribal enrollment requirements.

U.S. National, State & Territory Guidance

For U.S. states: start with the state vital records office for birth, death, marriage, and divorce certificates; county clerk or court for marriage licenses, divorce decrees, probate, and court records; county recorder/register of deeds for property records; and state archives or local libraries for older records.

For U.S. territories: start with the territory vital records office, civil registry, health department, court, land records office, territory archives, local libraries, and official territory government websites. Some forms, fees, access rules, and record names may differ from the 50 states.

For replacement documents: use official government offices. Be careful with third-party sites that charge extra fees or mimic government pages.

International / Country Records Guidance

Every country handles records differently. For international records, start with the country’s official civil registry, vital records office, national archives, local municipality, court, land registry, health ministry, justice ministry, religious/parish registry, cemetery office, embassy, or consulate.

Open International & Global Country Guidance

Common country terms may include civil registration, registry office, births/deaths/marriages office, family registry, household registry, population registry, national archives, parish register, land registry, cadastre, probate court, immigration office, or consular records.

  • Ask whether the record is public, restricted, sealed, or only available to the person/next of kin.
  • Ask whether you need an apostille, certified translation, notarization, consular legalization, or official seal.
  • If the record is for immigration, passport, citizenship, school, benefits, inheritance, or court use, ask the receiving agency exactly what version is accepted.
  • If you do not speak the language, ask about translation, interpreter help, embassy/consulate guidance, or a local legal aid office.

Safety, Privacy & Scam Protection

  • Use official government, court, archive, cemetery, or library websites first.
  • Be careful with sites that promise instant certified records for unusual fees.
  • Do not send Social Security numbers, passport numbers, bank information, or copies of ID to unknown people.
  • Some records contain sensitive information about adoption, paternity, abuse, tribal enrollment, immigration, health, or sealed court matters. Ask official offices about privacy rules.
  • For legal disputes, immigration matters, inheritance, custody, adoption, identity correction, or court use, contact legal aid, an attorney, official agency, embassy, or consulate.

Education Records, School Transcripts & Credentials

Education records are part of records help too. Visitors may need elementary, middle, high school, GED/high school equivalency, special education, trade school, college, university, graduate school, apprenticeship, closed school, territory, or international education records.

Start with the school, school district, registrar, state/territory education agency, adult education office, higher education agency, licensing agency, ministry of education, national qualifications office, or official country records custodian.

Quick Pathway

  1. Choose the record type.
  2. Identify where the event happened or where the record was created.
  3. Start with the official government office, court, archive, library, cemetery, military records office, or country/territory agency.
  4. Ask what proof, fee, form, identification, and processing time are required.
  5. Save the date contacted, person/office name, confirmation number, and next step.
